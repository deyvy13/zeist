<Callout type="success" title="Quick answer">
Yes. Revit produces lists with **schedules** and **material takeoffs**; AutoCAD, with **data extraction** and the COUNT command; and Civil 3D, with pipe and structure tables or pay items. What none of them does on its own is combine several models, use your inventory codes, add waste, round up to stock lengths and show what changed since the last list. That's what Dynamo or a plugin automates. At Zeist we build that plugin to order, in your purchasing format.
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="Glossary: the terms you'll read, in plain words">
- **Bill of materials (BOM)** — What has to be bought or fabricated: each material or part with its size, quantity and unit.
- **Schedule** — The Revit table that lists and counts model elements.
- **Material takeoff** — The Revit table that adds up areas and volumes by material.
- **Attribute** — A value stored inside an AutoCAD block, such as a code or a diameter.
- **Pay item** — The code Civil 3D assigns to each part to measure quantities.
- **Waste factor** — The percentage added for cuts, laps and breakage.
- **Plugin** — A program installed inside Revit, AutoCAD or Civil 3D that adds its own buttons to the top toolbar.

Written for project managers, field engineers, procurement and BIM coordinators.
</Callout>

## The list that always gets built by hand

Before every order, someone opens the model, counts, measures, copies to Excel, groups, adds waste and moves it into the purchasing format. When the design changes, it starts over, and nobody is quite sure what changed since the last order.

Revit, AutoCAD and Civil 3D already know how to count and measure. What's missing is turning that into the list your company actually uses. This guide shows what each program offers and how to automate the rest.

<TopicCard step="1" title="1. A bill of materials is not a quantity takeoff" time="~4 min">

Both come from the same model, but they answer different questions:

| | Bill of materials | Quantity takeoff |
|---|---|---|
| What it's for | Buying or fabricating | Estimating and paying for work |
| How it's grouped | By material, size and supplier code | By estimate line item |
| Units | Purchase units: sticks, sheets, bags, each | Item units: LF, SF, CY, lb |
| Waste | Added | Usually not |

That's why a quantity takeoff rarely works as a shopping list, or the other way around. We cover the takeoff in [automated quantity takeoff](/en/blog/tabla-de-metrados-automatica-revit-civil-3d).

<Callout type="info" title="In Civil 3D, «material list» means something else">
In cross sections, the material list is the cut and fill list computed with Compute Materials on the Analyze tab. It's stored in the sample line group and feeds the volume tables and the mass haul diagram. We cover it in [Civil 3D cross sections](/en/blog/secciones-transversales-civil-3d).
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="2" title="2. In Revit: schedules and material takeoffs" time="~6 min">

Both are on the **View** tab, Create panel:

- **Schedule/Quantities.** Lists the elements of a category with the fields you pick: type, size, length, count. Clear the option to itemize every instance and it groups identical elements and counts them.
- **Material Takeoff.** Adds up the area and volume of each material in the layers: wall concrete, floor finishes, insulation.

Schedules support **calculated values**: a formula can add waste or turn feet into sticks. You have to set that up in every schedule and every project.

**To get it into Excel**, File › Export › Reports › Schedule writes a delimited text file that Excel opens. It doesn't produce a formatted Excel workbook: Dynamo or a plugin does that. We cover it in [exporting Revit schedules to Excel](/en/blog/exportar-tablas-revit-excel-editar-parametros).

**The limit:** each schedule looks at one model. If architecture, structure and MEP are separate models, that's three lists someone combines by hand.

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="3" title="3. In AutoCAD: data extraction and COUNT" time="~6 min">

In AutoCAD, the list comes from **blocks**: their names and attributes.

**Data extraction** (`DATAEXTRACTION`). A wizard that reads one or more drawings, picks the blocks and their properties or attributes, and puts the result in a table in the drawing or in an external file, such as Excel or CSV. The extraction is saved as a template you can rerun, and the table flags when the data has changed.

**COUNT**, since AutoCAD 2022. It counts the blocks in the drawing, flags duplicated or exploded ones and inserts a table with the counts. Since AutoCAD 2023 it can also count within an area. The table doesn't update on its own: you save or regenerate.

<Callout type="warn" title="If you use AutoCAD LT">
AutoCAD LT doesn't include data extraction. The closest option is ATTEXT, which exports block attributes using a template, but it doesn't count or group.
</Callout>

**The list is only as good as the blocks.** If a valve block has no code, diameter and pressure attributes, there's nothing to pull. And if someone exploded a block, it drops out of the count.

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="4" title="4. In Civil 3D: pipes, structures and pay items" time="~5 min">

In water, sewer and storm networks, the parts are already objects with sizes:

- **Pipe and structure tables.** They list each pipe with diameter, material and length, and each manhole or inlet with its data.
- **Pay items (QTO).** On the Analyze tab, QTO Manager assigns codes to pipes and structures. Assign them in the parts list and every new part is tagged automatically. Then you generate a quantity report by pay item.

Set how length is measured, for example in 3D and between the inside faces of structures, and check the report against the objects: it doesn't always come out as expected.

**What's missing:** fittings such as bends, tees and reducers aren't always modeled, and the list doesn't know the stock length each pipe is bought in. We cover it in [Civil 3D pipe networks and fittings](/en/blog/redes-tuberias-civil-3d-accesorios).

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="5" title="5. What no built-in list does on its own" time="~5 min">

All of the above counts and measures. A real order needs more:

- **Several sources.** Revit models by discipline, AutoCAD drawings and Civil 3D networks in one list.
- **Your codes.** Inventory or supplier codes aren't in the model, and every company uses its own.
- **Stock lengths.** Rebar, pipe sold by the stick, full sheets: purchases round up to what the supplier sells.
- **Waste by material.** It isn't the same for rebar, tile or cable.
- **Purchase units.** Feet to sticks, cubic yards to bags, pounds to bars.
- **What changed.** Compare against the last list and order only the difference.

<Callout type="info" title="Signs it's worth automating">
You build the list by copying schedules into Excel for every order. You combine several models by hand. Change orders are worked out from memory. Or procurement found a shortage on site that the model already showed.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="6" title="6. Two ways to automate it: Dynamo or a plugin" time="~5 min">

**With Dynamo, in Revit and Civil 3D.** A script reads the elements and their parameters, groups them, applies formulas and writes an Excel file. It's quick to build and good for testing the rules. Plain AutoCAD, without Civil 3D, doesn't include Dynamo.

**With a plugin.** A button on the top toolbar that reads every model, assigns each element its code, applies the rules for stock lengths, waste and units, writes the list in your format and compares it with the previous one. It works in Revit, AutoCAD and Civil 3D, because all three have an API.
