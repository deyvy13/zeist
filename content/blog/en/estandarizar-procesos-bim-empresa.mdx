## The underlying problem: the knowledge lives in people

At most engineering offices, how things get done lives in the heads of whoever has been there longest. It works — until it doesn't.

It stops working when new people join. When two teams work on sections of the same project. When someone key leaves. When a client asks for a delivery format nobody has documented.

And then the symptom shows up: **two people solve the same problem two different ways**, and both believe theirs is correct. Because nobody ever agreed on which one was.

<TopicCard step="1" title="1. The 5 signs you don't have a standard" time="~5 min">

They show up before it becomes an expensive problem. If you recognize three or more, it's time to act.

**1. Onboarding a new hire depends on who they happen to sit next to.**
If learning gets passed on by osmosis, every new person inherits the habits of their desk neighbor — not the company's.

**2. Before delivery, someone "reviews everything" for hours.**
That review exists precisely because there's no confidence the process alone will produce good results. It's an expensive patch.

**3. Two teams' deliverables don't look alike.**
Same project, different section, different formats. The time spent reconciling before delivery is pure rework.

**4. The same questions keep coming up every week.**
"How is this named?" "Does this line item count or not?" If the same question gets asked every month, the answer should be written down somewhere.

**5. When someone leaves, they take critical knowledge with them.**
And for months afterward, decisions show up that nobody can explain.

<Callout type="warn">
Sign 5 is the most expensive one and the least anticipated. The cost of someone leaving isn't replacing the person — it's rebuilding what only they knew.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="2" title="2. Why rollouts fail" time="~7 min">

Almost all of them fail for the same reason, and it's avoidable.

### The failure pattern

1. Leadership decides to standardize.
2. A manual gets commissioned. Out comes a 60-80 page document.
3. It gets distributed by email. Maybe a presentation.
4. The first few weeks show some adoption.
5. A tight-deadline project comes along and everyone reverts to the old ways.
6. Six months later, the manual is outdated and nobody opens it.

### Why it happens

**It depends on everyone's memory.** A standard that requires remembering 80 pages while working against the clock doesn't get applied. Not out of bad faith — out of cognitive load.

**It adds work instead of removing it.** If following the standard means extra steps, it competes against the deadline. And the deadline always wins.

**There's no way to know if it's being followed.** Without verification, non-compliance stays invisible until it explodes on site.

**It was built without the people who have to use it.** The pushback is almost never against the standard itself — it's against being left out of the decision.

### What actually works

**A standard that verifies itself.** If a tool checks and flags what doesn't comply, nobody has to remember anything.

**A standard that removes work.** If it also auto-fixes what can be fixed, on top of verifying, the team adopts it without anyone pushing.

**A short standard.** Five rules that get followed beat eighty that get ignored.

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="3" title="3. What to standardize first" time="~8 min">

Ordered by impact and by how much resistance it triggers. Start at the top.

### 1. Naming and file structure
**Impact: high. Resistance: low.**

How files, layers, views, sheets, and objects are named. Nobody has an emotional attachment to this, and the payoff is immediate: everything becomes findable, tools can be built on top of it, and it's easy to verify.

**Always start here.**

### 2. Project templates
**Impact: high. Resistance: low.**

A new project starts with the right styles, layers, and configuration already in place. It's the cheapest way to standardize: **the standard is built in**, nobody has to remember it.

### 3. Measurement criteria
**Impact: very high. Resistance: medium.**

What's included in each line item, how it's rounded, what's excluded. Generates more discussion because it touches professional judgment — but it prevents the most rework.

**Key:** don't impose the criterion. Get the veterans in a room, let them argue it out, and document what they agree on.

### 4. Quality control before delivery
**Impact: high. Resistance: low if automated.**

A checklist of what must be satisfied before issuing. If it's manual, it gets skipped under pressure. If it's a button that produces a report, it gets used.

### 5. Review and approval workflow
**Impact: medium-high. Resistance: medium.**

Who reviews what, in what order, and how approval gets marked. It's more about management than modeling, and usually ends up last.

<Callout type="tip">
These five cover 80% of the problem. Everything else — families, catalogs, exchange protocols — can wait until year two.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="4" title="4. From document to tool (the critical point)" time="~7 min">

Here's the difference between a rollout that lasts and one that erodes.

**A standard that isn't automatically verified doesn't exist.** It exists on paper, not in the deliverables.

### The three states of a standard

**State 1 — Documented.** It's written down. It depends on each person reading it, remembering it, and applying it. **Shelf life: weeks.**

**State 2 — Built-in.** It's baked into templates and configurations. Nobody has to remember it because it comes pre-set. **Shelf life: years.**

**State 3 — Verified.** A tool checks the deliverable and flags what doesn't comply, before delivery happens. **Shelf life: permanent, and it keeps improving.**

### What "verified" means in practice

A button inside Civil 3D or Revit that, when clicked:

- Checks that naming follows the rule.
- Detects disallowed layers.
- Verifies required parameters are complete.
- Checks consistency between what's modeled and what's tabulated.
- Delivers a report of what's missing, ranked by severity.

**The most valuable side effect:** the team learns the standard by using it. Nobody reads the manual, but everyone understands the warning that pops up.

<Callout type="success">
Practical rule: for every page of manual you write, invest twice the effort in making the rule built-in or verified. The document is the backup, not the mechanism.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="5" title="5. How to handle team resistance" time="~7 min">

Resistance is predictable and largely legitimate. Ignoring it guarantees failure.

### Where it actually comes from

**It's not resistance to order.** Almost nobody defends chaos. What people push back against is:

- Having it decided without consulting them, after years of doing it a way that works.
- Adding steps while the deadline doesn't move.
- Having lived through previous rollouts that got abandoned after three months.
- The feeling that their professional judgment is being questioned.

### The four things that work

**1. Have the standard remove work, not add it.**
If the verification tool also fixes what can be fixed, the team adopts it without anyone insisting. It's the most powerful lever.

**2. Involve the veterans in defining it.**
Not everyone — the three or four with the most technical weight. Let them argue the criterion, disagree, and reach an agreement. What they defined, they defend.

**3. Pilot before rollout.**
One project, one team, three months. With results in hand, the conversation with everyone else changes completely.

**4. Show the number, not the rule.**
"We went from 4 revisions per document to 2" convinces more than "you have to follow the standard."

### What guarantees rejection

- Imposing it by email with no prior conversation.
- Starting with the measurement criteria, the part that touches professional judgment the most.
- Not allowing transition time while demanding the same deadlines.
- Leadership not applying it to itself.

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="6" title="6. A 90-day rollout plan" time="~8 min">

With production running, because nobody is going to stop projects for this.

### Days 1-15 — Agreement, not imposition

- Meeting with the 3-4 most senior technical profiles.
- On the table: the five areas from module 3. Pick **one**.
- Write the agreed rule on **one page**. No more.
- Define who's responsible and how many protected hours per week they get.

**Deliverable:** one page signed by the people who'll use it.

### Days 16-45 — Build it in before enforcing it

- Bake the rule into project templates.
- Build or commission automatic verification of that rule.
- Test it against two already-finished projects to calibrate.

**Why finished projects:** it validates the tool without pressure and shows the team how many deviations existed, without singling anyone out on a live project.

### Days 46-75 — Pilot on a real project

- Apply it to **one** new project, with one team.
- Biweekly 30-minute meeting: what's failing, what's excessive, what's missing.
- Adjust the rule based on what you learn. **It's expected to change.**

### Days 76-90 — Measure and decide

- Compare against an equivalent project without the standard.
- Metrics: pre-delivery review findings, time spent reconciling, revisions needed for approval.
- With the number in hand, decide whether to roll it out further and whether to tackle the second area.

<Callout type="warn">
The mistakes that ruin this plan: (1) starting with more than one area at once, (2) skipping the pilot and going straight to the whole company, (3) not protecting the lead's hours — if they have to do it "whenever they can," it won't get done.
</Callout>

**By the end of the 90 days**, you have one truly standardized area, a tool that sustains it, and your own data to justify the next one. That's infinitely more useful than a complete manual nobody follows.

</TopicCard>
