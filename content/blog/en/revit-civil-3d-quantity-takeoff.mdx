<Callout type="success" title="Quick answer">
Yes. Revit and Civil 3D already compute quantities: Revit with schedules and material takeoffs, Civil 3D with section volumes and pay items. What they don't do on their own is turn them into the **bill of quantities**: map each element to its line item, write the backup with every measurement, combine several models and use your client's format. A plugin that writes the spreadsheet in Excel from the model automates that. At Zeist we build it to order.
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="Glossary: the terms you'll read, in plain words">
- **Quantity takeoff** — Measuring the work in a design: lengths, areas, volumes, weights and counts.
- **Line item** — Each row of the estimate, with its code, description and unit. Also called a pay item or bid item.
- **Bill of quantities (BOQ)** — The table listing every line item with its quantity.
- **Backup** — The detail behind each quantity: which elements were measured, and how.
- **Schedule** — The Revit table that lists and measures model elements.
- **Plugin** — A program installed inside Revit or Civil 3D that adds its own buttons to the top toolbar.

Written for estimators, project engineers, reviewers and BIM coordinators.
</Callout>

## The step that's always done by hand

The model already knows how much concrete is in every beam and how many cubic yards of cut each stretch of road has. Yet the bill of quantities is still built by hand: export schedules, copy them to Excel, rearrange them by line item and write the backup row by row. And for every design version, it all happens again.

This guide separates what Revit and Civil 3D already compute from what's missing to get the deliverable, and shows how to automate that stretch.

<TopicCard step="1" title="1. What a quantity takeoff deliverable includes" time="~5 min">

A takeoff deliverable has three parts:

1. **The line items**, in estimate order: code, description and unit.
2. **The backup** for each item: the elements measured, how many times they repeat and their dimensions, with a subtotal per row.
3. **The summary**, with the total for each item, which goes into the estimate or the summary of quantities on the plans.

Items follow a structure: CSI MasterFormat codes on building projects, the agency's pay item list on transportation and site work. Many clients also require their own spreadsheet format.

So the deliverable isn't an exported schedule: it's a document with an order, a measurement rule and a format. A shopping list is something else; we cover it in [bill of materials from Revit and AutoCAD](/en/blog/revit-autocad-bill-of-materials).

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="2" title="2. Where the quantities come from" time="~5 min">

| Source | What it measures well |
|---|---|
| **Revit** | Concrete, wall and floor areas, finishes, doors and windows, modeled rebar |
| **Civil 3D** | Cut and fill from sections or between surfaces, pipe lengths, quantities by pay item |
| **AutoCAD** | Polyline areas and lengths, and block counts |
| **By hand** | What isn't modeled: mobilization, layout, testing, lump-sum items |

Each source delivers quantities its own way. None delivers the bill of quantities. We cover Civil 3D volumes in [Civil 3D cross sections](/en/blog/civil-3d-cross-sections).

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="3" title="3. From the model to the spreadsheet: the manual stretch" time="~5 min">

What gets done by hand today, for every version:

- **Exporting** Revit schedules, which come out as delimited text, and Civil 3D volumes.
- **Mapping each element to its line item.** If the model doesn't carry the item code on each element, it's done from memory.
- **Writing the backup:** one row per element, with count, length, width and height.
- **Applying the measurement rules**, such as deducting openings or converting units.
- **Formatting it for the client**, with their columns, headers and totals.
- **Combining disciplines** into one summary.

It's the most time-consuming part and the one that introduces the most errors, because every copy is a chance to get something wrong.

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="4" title="4. Why the numbers don't add up" time="~5 min">

When the spreadsheet and the model disagree, it's almost always one of these:

- **Elements with no line item**, or the wrong one, left out of the total.
- **Volumes counted twice**, for example where beams, columns and slabs meet. In Revit, the join order decides which element gets each volume.
- **Different units and rounding** between the model, Excel and the estimate.
- **Measurement rules that differ** from the client's.
- **Design changes that never reached the spreadsheet**, because one tab was updated and another wasn't.

<Callout type="warn" title="The change order nobody can back up">
On site, a quantity overrun is justified by comparing what was built with the design quantities. If the design takeoff was assembled by hand and can't be traced back to the model, that comparison turns into an argument.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="5" title="5. What can be automated" time="~5 min">

The model already does the math. What gets automated is the stretch to the spreadsheet:

- **Mapping line items by rule**, through a lookup table: category, type or material in the model, and its line item.
- **Writing the backup** for each item from the model elements, with their dimensions.
- **Applying the measurement rules** your firm or client uses.
- **Combining Revit, Civil 3D and manual items** in one Excel workbook, in the client's format.
- **Comparing versions** to show which items went up or down, and because of which elements.
- **Checking before submitting:** elements without an item, zero quantities or duplicated items.

<Callout type="info" title="Signs it's worth automating">
Every design version means rebuilding the spreadsheet. The backup is written by hand. Disciplines arrive in different formats. Or a review comment forces you to redo the whole estimate.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="6" title="6. Dynamo or a plugin" time="~5 min">

**With Dynamo**, in Revit or Civil 3D, a script reads the elements, groups them by a line item parameter and writes an Excel file. It's quick to build and good for testing the lookup table.

**With a plugin**, the full deliverable comes out with one button: every discipline, the backup, the summary, the client's format and the comparison with the previous version.
