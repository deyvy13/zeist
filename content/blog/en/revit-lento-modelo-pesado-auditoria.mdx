<Callout type="success" title="Quick answer">
**A slow Revit model** is almost always caused by what's in the model, not by the computer. The most common causes are **imported CAD** instead of linked, **in-place families**, **hundreds of unresolved warnings**, **large and modified groups**, **unused families and views**, and a **central file that's never compacted**. Fix them in a clear order: remove or link the CAD, resolve warnings, purge unused, and compact the central. To keep it from happening again, firms use a **model audit plugin** that measures these indicators every week and flags problems before the model degrades.
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="Glossary: the terms you'll read, in plain language">
- **Central model** — The shared file the whole team works on. Each person works on a local copy and syncs.
- **Sync** — Sending your changes to the central model and receiving everyone else's.
- **Import vs. link CAD** — Importing puts the drawing inside the model. Linking just references it from outside. Importing is far heavier.
- **In-place family** — An object modeled directly inside the project instead of loaded as a reusable family. Convenient and very heavy.
- **Warning** — The message Revit raises when something isn't resolved: overlapping walls, unenclosed rooms, duplicate elements.
- **Purge Unused** — The command that deletes families, types and materials the model doesn't use.
- **Compact Central** — An option when saving or syncing that rewrites the file and removes wasted space.
- **Plugin** — A program installed inside Revit that adds its own buttons to the toolbar at the top.

No programming jargon. This is written for BIM coordinators and project managers.
</Callout>

## The cost that never shows up in a report

Nobody logs how long they wait on Revit. But do the math:

- Opening the model: 5 minutes, twice a day.
- Syncing: 4 minutes, five times a day.
- Heavy views, switching sheets, regenerating: another 15 scattered minutes.

That's about **55 minutes per person per day**. On a team of 10, **more than a full workday lost every day**. Not counting unexpected crashes that take unsaved work with them.

The serious part is that the model **degrades gradually**. Nobody notices the day it started: an imported DWG here, an in-place family there, a hundred warnings nobody reviews. By the time the problem is obvious, it's big.

<TopicCard step="1" title="1. The 7 reasons Revit runs slow" time="~7 min">

Ranked by typical impact, highest first.

**1. Imported CAD.** The number one cause. A survey or architectural drawing imported — worse if exploded — brings thousands of lines, layers and styles into the model. **Link it instead of importing it**, and if it's only needed in one view, link it to that view only.

**2. In-place families.** Handy for a one-off, but heavy and not reusable. Copying an in-place family ten times is ten times the weight.

**3. Unresolved warnings.** Overlapping walls, duplicate rooms, identical elements in the same place. Hundreds or thousands of warnings make Revit recompute more and hurt stability.

**4. Large and modified groups.** Groups are useful for repeating units, but nested groups or groups edited one by one are among the most expensive objects to regenerate.

**5. Unused content.** Families, types, materials and fill patterns someone loaded and never used. You don't see them, but they still load.

**6. Too many views.** Working views nobody deleted, 3D views with heavy visual styles, views with unlimited depth.

**7. Central file never compacted.** Over time the file accumulates wasted space. Compacting periodically shrinks it without touching content.

<Callout type="warn" title="Before you blame the computer">
Buying new hardware for a badly built model means paying twice. A faster machine helps, but a model with imported CAD and thousands of warnings will be slow on any computer. Diagnose the model first.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="2" title="2. How to diagnose a slow model today" time="~6 min">

Without any external tools, you can have a diagnosis within an hour:

1. **Open with Audit.** When opening the file, check *Audit*. Revit checks and repairs internal inconsistencies. Do it on a copy.
2. **Count warnings.** On the Manage tab, review warnings and note how many there are and of what type. The most frequent type is your first target.
3. **Find imported CAD.** In Manage Links, list everything that's imported. Each one is a candidate to link or delete.
4. **Count in-place families.** Filter for them and note how many there are and where.
5. **Measure file size.** Write it down today. Without a baseline you won't know if you improved.
6. **Review groups.** How many group types and how many instances.

<Callout type="info" title="The number that matters most">
It isn't the file size itself: it's **how it changes over time**. A model that grows steadily with the project is healthy. One that jumps — 300 MB to 500 MB in a week — had something imported or duplicated that week, and you want to know what.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="3" title="3. What to fix first (order matters)" time="~6 min">

The right order saves hours: biggest impact and lowest risk first.

**1. Link or remove imported CAD.** The biggest win with the least risk. If the CAD is needed, replace it with a link.

**2. Resolve the most frequent warnings.** Usually two or three types make up most of them. Fixing those types drops the count dramatically.

**3. Purge Unused.** Run it several times: deleting one family sometimes frees another that depended on it.

**4. Convert repeated in-place families** into loadable families.

**5. Delete working views** that aren't on any sheet. Carefully, and with the team's agreement.

**6. Compact the central file.**

<Callout type="warn" title="Always work on a copy first">
Purging, deleting views and compacting aren't easily undone. Work on a copy, confirm everything is fine, and only then apply it to the central with the team disconnected.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="4" title="4. Why manual cleanup doesn't last" time="~5 min">

Almost every office has done a big model cleanup at some point. The model improves. Three months later it's the same or worse.

The reason is simple: **cleanup is an event, decay is a process**. As long as nobody measures, people keep importing CAD in a rush, creating in-place families because it's faster, and leaving warnings for later.

What works isn't cleaning harder, it's **measuring always**. When the team sees every Monday that warnings went from 200 to 450, and who created them, behavior changes on its own.

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="5" title="5. How a model audit plugin works" time="~8 min">

An audit plugin turns model health into a **metric that measures itself**, like any other project KPI.

**What it measures on every run:**

- File size and its trend.
- Number of warnings, grouped by type.
- Imported and linked CAD.
- In-place families, unused families and the heaviest families.
- Groups: types, instances and nested groups.
- Views not placed on any sheet.
- Loaded linked models.

**What it does with that:**

1. **Computes a health score** for the model, against thresholds your office defines.
2. **Keeps the history** week by week, to see trends and catch the jump.
3. **Produces a report** for the BIM coordinator: what got worse, where and since when.
4. **Offers safe one-click fixes** — purge unused, list candidate views to delete — always with human review before anything is deleted.

<TopicCard step="6" title="6. How to roll it out in your office" time="~5 min">

**Week 1 · Baseline.** Measure your active models: size, warnings, imported CAD, in-place families. That's your starting point.

**Week 2 · Set thresholds.** What a healthy model means for your office: maximum warnings, zero imported CAD, how many in-place families are tolerated.

**Week 3 · Guided cleanup.** Fix things in the order from section 3, on copies, with the team informed.

**Week 4 · Automated audit.** Turn on weekly measurement and share the report with the team. From then on, the model doesn't degrade without someone seeing it.

</TopicCard>
