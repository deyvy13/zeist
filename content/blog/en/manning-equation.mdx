<Callout type="success" title="Quick answer">
Manning's equation gives the velocity of water in a channel or in a pipe that isn't under pressure: V = (1/n) · R^(2/3) · S^(1/2) in SI units, or V = (1.49/n) · R^(2/3) · S^(1/2) in US units. Discharge is Q = V · A. A concrete channel 1 m wide, 0.50 m deep, on a 0.2 % slope carries 0.59 m³/s (20.9 cfs).
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="Glossary: the terms you'll read, in plain English">
- **n (roughness coefficient)** — How rough the material is. The higher it is, the more it slows the water down.
- **Flow area (A)** — The part of the cross-section the water fills.
- **Wetted perimeter (P)** — The edge of the section the water touches: bottom and walls, not the free surface.
- **Hydraulic radius (R)** — Flow area divided by wetted perimeter.
- **Slope (S)** — How much the bottom drops per unit of length, in ft/ft or m/m.
- **Flow depth** — How deep the water is.
- **Uniform flow** — The depth doesn't change along the reach. It's the condition Manning's equation assumes.
</Callout>

## What does Manning's equation say?

```
SI:  V = (1/n) · R^(2/3) · S^(1/2)      V in m/s, R in m
US:  V = (1.49/n) · R^(2/3) · S^(1/2)   V in ft/s, R in ft
Q = V · A
```

- **V** is the mean velocity.
- **n** is the roughness coefficient of the material. It's the same number in both unit systems.
- **R** is the hydraulic radius: R = A / P.
- **S** is the bottom slope as a ratio. A 0.2 % slope is 0.002.
- **Q** is the discharge, and **A** the flow area.

The 1.49 (more precisely 1.486) only converts units. Manning applies to **uniform free-surface flow**: channels, gutters, sanitary sewers and storm drains. It doesn't apply to pressurized pipes, which use Hazen-Williams or Darcy-Weisbach.

## How do you solve an example step by step?

A rectangular concrete channel, **1.00 m (3.28 ft) wide**, flowing **0.50 m (1.64 ft) deep**, on a **0.2 % slope** (S = 0.002), with **n = 0.015**. How much water does it carry?

| Step | SI | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Flow area | A = 1.00 × 0.50 | 0.50 m² |
| 2. Wetted perimeter | P = 1.00 + 2 × 0.50 | 2.00 m |
| 3. Hydraulic radius | R = 0.50 / 2.00 | 0.25 m |
| 4. Powers | R^(2/3) = 0.397 and S^(1/2) = 0.0447 | — |
| 5. Velocity | V = (1 / 0.015) × 0.397 × 0.0447 | 1.18 m/s |
| 6. Discharge | Q = 1.18 × 0.50 | 0.59 m³/s |

The same channel in US units: A = 5.38 ft², P = 6.56 ft, R = 0.82 ft, V = (1.49 / 0.015) × 0.82^(2/3) × 0.0447 = 3.89 ft/s, and **Q = 20.9 cfs**. That's the same 0.59 m³/s.

One more check: the **Froude number**, F = V / √(g · y) in a rectangular channel. Here F = 1.18 / √(9.81 × 0.50) = 0.53. It's below 1, so the flow is **subcritical**: calm and stable.

## What n value should you use for concrete, PVC or earth?

These are the same values the [Manning calculator](/en/tools/manning-calculator) uses:

| Material | Design n | Typical range |
|---|---|---|
| PVC | 0.010 | 0.009 – 0.011 |
| Concrete pipe | 0.013 | 0.011 – 0.015 |
| Cast iron | 0.013 | 0.010 – 0.014 |
| Corrugated metal | 0.024 | 0.021 – 0.030 |
| Concrete-lined channel | 0.015 | 0.013 – 0.016 |
| Masonry | 0.025 | 0.017 – 0.030 |
| Clean earth | 0.022 | 0.018 – 0.025 |
| Earth with grass | 0.027 | 0.022 – 0.033 |
| Rock | 0.035 | 0.025 – 0.040 |
| Natural stream | 0.030 | 0.025 – 0.033 |

The material matters a lot. The same channel dug in **clean earth** (n = 0.022) carries **0.40 m³/s**, 32 % less. With **grass** (n = 0.027), 0.33 m³/s.

Use the design value, not the brand-new-pipe value: over the years you get sediment, joints and wear. If your agency or your state's design manual sets an n, use that one.

## How does it work for a pipe that isn't flowing full?

With the pipe **full**, the geometry is simple: A = π · D² / 4, P = π · D, so **R = D / 4**. A **300 mm (about 12 in)** concrete pipe (n = 0.013) on a **0.5 %** slope:

- R = 0.30 / 4 = 0.075 m
- V = (1 / 0.013) × 0.075^(2/3) × 0.005^(1/2) = **0.97 m/s** (3.2 ft/s)
- Q = 0.97 × 0.0707 = **68 L/s** (2.4 cfs)

With the pipe **partly full**, area and perimeter depend on the angle the water surface makes, and the math gets longer. One result surprises people: maximum discharge does **not** happen with the pipe full. It happens at **94 % of the diameter**, and it's 7.6 % higher. As the pipe fills completely, the wetted perimeter grows faster than the area and the water slows down.

## How do you find the depth if you know the discharge?

That's the reverse problem, and the most common one in design: you know how much water arrives and need to know how deep it will run. That depth is the **normal depth**.

Manning's equation can't be solved directly for depth, because area and perimeter both depend on it. You solve it by trial: guess a depth, compute the discharge, adjust until it matches. In the example channel, for **0.40 m³/s** the normal depth is **0.375 m**, at 1.07 m/s.

The calculator does this in **Normal depth** mode, for circular pipes and rectangular, trapezoidal and triangular channels.

## What are the most common mistakes?

1. **Slope as a percentage.** Typing 0.2 instead of 0.002 multiplies the discharge by 10: the example channel would carry 5.92 m³/s.
2. **Using the diameter as the hydraulic radius.** For a full pipe, R is D/4. With R = D, the 300 mm pipe would carry 172 L/s instead of 68.
3. **Mixing units.** In feet the factor is 1.49. Leave it out and the discharge comes out 33 % low.
4. **Picking the catalog n for new pipe.** Design is for the service life, not day one.
5. **Applying it to a pressurized pipe.** Manning is for free-surface flow.
6. **Assuming a full pipe is maximum capacity.** The peak is at 94 % of the diameter. If more water arrives than that, the pipe surcharges and Manning no longer applies.
