<Callout type="success" title="Quick answer">
A **BIM audit** is a structured review of how a firm produces its projects in BIM. It has two parts: the **model audit**, which checks the technical quality of the files (naming, coordinates, warnings, information, consistency between model, drawings and takeoffs), and the **process audit**, which measures where hours are lost, which tasks repeat and which standards are missing. The result is a **report with prioritized findings and an action plan**: what to fix, what to standardize and what to automate first. It's worth doing before a major bid, when review comments keep repeating, or when models become slow and inconsistent.
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="Glossary: the terms you'll read, in plain language">
- **Model audit** — Reviewing the files: whether they're well built, well named and carry the right information.
- **Process audit** — Reviewing how the team works: what tasks it does, how long they take, and what repeats.
- **BIM standard** — The firm's written rules: naming, file structure, templates, information levels.
- **BIM execution plan** — The document defining how a specific project will be delivered.
- **Level of information** — What data each model element must carry at each project stage.
- **Shared coordinates** — The system that makes every model in a project line up in the same place.
- **Automation** — Turning a repetitive manual task into a button, with Dynamo or a plugin.

No programming jargon. This is written for managers, design leads and BIM coordinators.
</Callout>

## Why almost no firm knows what its way of working costs

Every firm knows what it bills for a project. Very few know how many of the hours behind it were **work that got repeated, redone or lost**.

It isn't carelessness: those hours don't appear in any report. They hide in phrases like *"the model was slow"*, *"we had to redo the takeoff"*, *"the reviewer sent the drawings back"*, or *"the person who knew how was on another project"*.

A BIM audit brings those hours into the open and turns them into decisions.

<TopicCard step="1" title="1. Model audit vs. process audit" time="~5 min">

They're two different things, and it pays not to confuse them, because they answer different questions.

**The model audit** answers: *are our files well made?* It checks technical quality: whether they meet the standard, whether information is complete, whether the model matches the drawings and the takeoff. Much of it can be **automated** and repeated every week.

**The process audit** answers: *are we working as efficiently as we could?* It measures how long each task takes, which ones repeat on every project, where work gets redone and what depends on a single person. It needs conversations with the team and observation of real work.

<Callout type="info" title="The one that creates the most value">
The model audit finds errors. The process audit finds **the cause** of those errors and the lost hours. If you can only do one, start with the process: it tells you what to automate and where the return is.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="2" title="2. Checklist: what the model audit reviews" time="~8 min">

This is the list we use as a baseline. Adapt it to your standard and project types.

**Structure and naming**
- File names per the project's or agency's standard.
- Naming of views, sheets, levels, layers and families.
- Folder structure and links between models.

**Location and coordinates**
- Shared coordinates set up and consistent across disciplines.
- Project georeferenced where required.
- Levels and grids identical across every model.

**Technical health**
- Number and type of unresolved warnings.
- Imported CAD instead of linked.
- In-place families, unused content, file size. We cover this in [slow Revit model: how to audit it](/en/blog/revit-lento-modelo-pesado-auditoria).

**Information**
- Required parameters complete per the requested level of information.
- Cost, classification or asset codes where required.
- Empty or inconsistent parameters.

**Consistency**
- Drawings generated from the current model version.
- Takeoffs matching the model.
- Clashes between disciplines resolved or documented.

<Callout type="warn" title="The item that prevents the most review comments">
Consistency between model, drawings and takeoffs. It's what reviewers check most, and what breaks most when the project changes.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="3" title="3. Checklist: what the process audit reviews" time="~7 min">

**Time per task**
- How long each stage takes: modeling, takeoffs, sheets, review, submittal.
- Which tasks repeat on every project with the same rules.

**Rework**
- What gets redone in every design revision, and why.
- How many review rounds each submittal had, and of what kind.

**Standards**
- Whether a written standard exists and whether it's built into templates.
- Whether every project is delivered the same way or depends on who does it.

**People and knowledge**
- Which tasks depend on a single person.
- What happens when that person is out.

**Tools**
- What's done by hand that the software already solves.
- What's done by hand that could be automated with Dynamo or a plugin.

<TopicCard step="4" title="4. What you should receive at the end" time="~5 min">

An audit that ends in a PDF with fifty unordered findings is useless. Here's what it should deliver:

**A diagnosis with numbers.** Hours per task, review rounds, model health. Without numbers, there's no investment decision.

**Findings prioritized by impact and effort.** What to fix this week, this quarter, and later.

**An action plan on three fronts:**
- **Fix:** specific errors in current models.
- **Standardize:** rules that still need writing or building into templates.
- **Automate:** repetitive tasks that are candidates for Dynamo or a plugin, with the estimated savings for each.

**A baseline to measure against.** Today's indicators, to compare three months from now.

<Callout type="success" title="The question the audit has to answer">
*"If we could only change three things this quarter, which would give us back the most hours?"* If the report doesn't answer that clearly, it isn't finished.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="5" title="5. When your firm needs a BIM audit" time="~5 min">

These are the clearest signals. If you recognize two or more, it's time:

- **Review comments keep repeating** on submittals, mostly about formatting or quantities.
- **Models are slow** or get corrupted often.
- **Every project is delivered differently**, depending on who did it.
- **There's a major bid** or a new client with BIM requirements.
- **New people joined** and take months to produce like the rest.
- **The team works overtime** every submittal week.
- **You want to automate** but don't know where to start.

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="6" title="6. How to do it without slowing projects" time="~5 min">

**Week 1 · Scope.** Pick two or three representative projects and the disciplines involved.

**Week 2 · Model review.** Run the model checklist, with automated tools wherever possible.

**Week 3 · Process.** Short interviews with the team and observation of a real submittal. Measure times.

**Week 4 · Report and plan.** Prioritized findings, the three-front plan and a baseline.

After that, the model audit becomes **automated and weekly**, and the process audit is repeated once or twice a year to measure progress.

</TopicCard>
