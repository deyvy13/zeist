<Callout type="success" title="Quick answer">
A Civil 3D corridor is the 3D model of a road, channel or pad. It's built from three pieces: an alignment, a profile and an assembly, which is the typical section made of subassemblies. Civil 3D applies the assembly along the centerline at a set spacing, called frequency, and adapts it with targets: surfaces, alignments and profiles that control widths, elevations and slopes. The corridor produces the finished-grade surface, the cross sections and the cut and fill volumes. If a typical section doesn't exist, you can build it in Subassembly Composer.
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="Glossary: the terms you'll read, in plain words">
- **Alignment** — The road centerline in plan: tangents, curves and spirals, with their stations.
- **Profile** — The centerline in elevation: the road's grade at each station.
- **Assembly** — The typical section: lanes, shoulders, curbs, ditches and side slopes, put together as pieces.
- **Subassembly** — Each piece of the assembly: a lane, a curb, a side slope.
- **Frequency** — How often Civil 3D applies the typical section along the centerline.
- **Target** — Something in the drawing the section "follows": a surface to tie the slope into, an edge that sets a width, a profile that sets an elevation.
- **Daylight** — The point where the cut or fill slope meets existing ground.

Written for civil engineers who already design in Civil 3D and want to understand corridors in depth.
</Callout>

## Why the corridor is where time gets lost

On a roadway project, almost everything you deliver comes from the corridor: the finished-grade surface, the cross sections, the volumes and much of the plan set. When the corridor is built well, a profile change flows through on its own. When it isn't, every change means checking by hand for holes, slopes that don't reach the ground and sections that don't add up.

This guide explains how it really works, with the names you see on screen, and how to fix the errors people search for most.

<TopicCard step="1" title="1. The three pieces of a corridor" time="~5 min">

At a minimum, a corridor needs:

- **An alignment**, which sets where the centerline runs.
- **A design profile** on that alignment, which sets its elevation. How to build it is in [Civil 3D profiles and profile views](/en/blog/civil-3d-profile-view).
- **An assembly**, which defines the section on each side of the centerline.

The alignment and profile make up the corridor's **baseline**. A single corridor can have several baselines: one per street, per ramp, or per edge of an intersection.

Each baseline is divided into **regions**: station ranges that use one assembly. If the section changes, say from a hillside cut to an urban stretch with curbs, you add another region with another assembly.

<Callout type="tip" title="Before you create the corridor">
Make sure the design profile covers the full range. Where the profile stops, so does the corridor: the region comes up short, and it's easy to miss until you look at the sections.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="2" title="2. Assemblies and subassemblies: the typical section" time="~6 min">

You build the assembly in plan, at any point in the drawing, by attaching subassemblies on each side of a baseline marker. Civil 3D ships a tool palette with dozens of ready-made subassemblies. The most used:

| Subassembly | What it models |
|---|---|
| BasicLane | A lane with width, slope and depth |
| LaneSuperelevationAOR | A lane that follows the alignment's superelevation |
| BasicShoulder | A shoulder |
| BasicCurbAndGutter | A curb and gutter |
| BasicSideSlopeCutDitch | A cut slope with a ditch, or a fill slope, to the surface |
| LinkSlopeToSurface | A fixed-slope link that seeks a surface |
| ConditionalCutOrFill | Picks one branch or another depending on cut or fill |

**Cut and fill, each with its own slope: the conditional assembly.** With ConditionalCutOrFill, the assembly can have one branch for cut, with a ditch and its slope, and another for fill, with a flatter slope. At each station, Civil 3D uses whichever branch matches where the ground is.

**If the typical section isn't on the palette**, you build it in **Subassembly Composer**, the program that ships with Civil 3D for designing your own subassemblies with a flowchart, no code required. You export a .pkt file and import it into Civil 3D. It's the route for special ditches, retaining walls or a [Civil 3D duct bank](/en/blog/civil-3d-duct-bank).

<Callout type="info" title="Codes are everything">
Every subassembly carries codes on its points, links and shapes: Top for the finished surface and Datum for subgrade, for example. Those codes drive the corridor surface, the section labels and the materials used for volumes. A firm that keeps the same codes across all its assemblies can automate almost everything downstream.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="3" title="3. Regions, frequency and targets" time="~7 min">

**Frequency.** Civil 3D doesn't model the corridor continuously: it applies the typical section at stations, at a set spacing along tangents, curves and spirals, plus at the points where the alignment and profile geometry changes. Between one station and the next, it joins the points with straight lines. That's why:

- With low frequency on curves, edges look faceted and volumes are approximate.
- With very high frequency everywhere, the corridor takes longer to rebuild and the drawing gets heavier.

A common practice is to use more stations on curves than on tangents, and always include the geometry points.

**Targets.** A subassembly can follow something in the drawing instead of using a fixed value. You assign them in the corridor properties, in Target Mapping, and there are three kinds:

| Target type | What it controls | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Surface | Where a slope ties in, up or down | Existing ground, for daylight |
| Width or offset | How far a lane or shoulder extends | An edge alignment, a polyline, a feature line |
| Slope or elevation | The elevation a point reaches | An edge profile, a feature line, a 3D polyline |

An example: a widening ahead of a bridge is modeled with an offset edge alignment. The lane follows that edge and the width changes on its own, with no extra region.

<Callout type="warn" title="An empty target doesn't warn you">
If a target is left as "None", the subassembly falls back to its default value or stops drawing that part, with no clear message. It's the most common reason a side slope doesn't show up.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="4" title="4. The corridor surface and its boundary" time="~6 min">

The corridor surface is the finished project. You create it in the corridor properties, on the Surfaces tab:

- Add **link codes**: Top for finished grade or Datum for subgrade, depending on what it's for.
- If needed, add **feature lines** as breaklines, so the triangulation respects edges and gutters.
- Define the **boundary**, so the triangulation doesn't bridge the gaps between ramps or cross outside the slopes.

**The automatic boundary** is added on the Boundaries tab, using the daylight feature line. It only works if daylight exists on both sides of the corridor along the whole range. If one side has no slope, say next to a retaining wall, draw the boundary as a polyline and add it instead.

**With the corridor surface** you compare volumes against existing ground, draw [the project's contour lines](/en/blog/civil-3d-contours-google-earth) and take the design into other programs, as in [moving Civil 3D topography into Revit](/en/blog/civil-3d-to-revit-surface-coordinates).

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="5" title="5. Intersections: the hardest case" time="~5 min">

At an intersection, the crown, edges, curbs and curb returns all change at once. Civil 3D includes the **Create Intersection** wizard, which takes two alignments with their profiles and creates:

- The intersection object.
- The offset alignments for the edges and the curb-return alignments, with their profiles.
- The assemblies and corridor regions in the intersection area.

What the wizard doesn't decide is the design criteria: which road holds its crown, how the corner drains, what radius to use. That's still engineering. And every radius or profile change means reviewing the intersection again.

<Callout type="info" title="When there are dozens">
On a subdivision with dozens of streets and intersections, building them one by one takes days. It's one of the processes most worth automating.
</Callout>

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="6" title="6. The most-searched errors" time="~7 min">

- **The corridor is out of date.** You changed the profile or a target and the corridor didn't change. Prospector flags it as out of date: rebuild it, or turn on Rebuild Automatic, which makes every change slow in large drawings.
- **The side slope doesn't reach the ground.** The surface target isn't assigned, the existing surface doesn't cover that area, or the slope has a maximum width that falls short.
- **Holes or spikes in the surface.** Regions that don't meet, too few stations on curves, or no boundary: the triangulation connects points it shouldn't.
- **The corridor surface isn't showing.** It was created without link codes, so it's empty; the boundary clips all of it; or its style displays nothing.
- **The width doesn't change at the widening.** The width target is assigned to a different subassembly, or the edge alignment doesn't cover the whole region.
- **The section jumps between regions.** Switching assemblies without a transition leaves steps. Model the transition with width and elevation targets.
- **The sections don't show the corridor.** The corridor isn't among the sample line sources. We explain it in [Civil 3D cross sections](/en/blog/civil-3d-cross-sections).
- **The corridor is very slow.** Too many regions, too many stations everywhere, or a very dense surface as a target. Splitting the project into several corridors by segment helps.

</TopicCard>

<TopicCard step="7" title="7. Volumes, and what's worth automating" time="~5 min">

Volumes come out of the corridor in two ways:

- **From sections:** sample lines, materials and a volume table, by the average end area method. Step by step in [Civil 3D cross sections](/en/blog/civil-3d-cross-sections).
- **From surfaces:** comparing the corridor surface against existing ground in a volume surface.

**What eats the most hours in a firm**, and can be automated:

- Creating the corridors for a subdivision with many streets, with the same typical section and its regions.
- Assigning targets in bulk, without opening the dialog street by street.
- Exporting corridor points by code and station to Excel, for staking or quantities.
- Checking, before plan production, that every corridor is rebuilt, has a boundary and has no empty regions.
